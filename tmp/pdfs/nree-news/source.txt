Abstract
Halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) offer tunable optoelectronic 
properties and low fabrication costs; however, their industrial 
deployment is constrained by tightly coupled dependencies among 
composition, processing and device architecture. Artificial intelligence 
(AI) has been widely applied across the PSC research cycle, but its impact  
remains uneven: models accelerate local optimization within data-rich 
regimes yet do not generalize across compositions, fabrication 
environments or device architectures. This unevenness reflects the 
difficulty of representing the coupled, cross-scale relationships that 
govern PSC behaviour rather than a limitation of modelling capability 
alone. We examine AI applications across materials discovery, device 
engineering, process optimization and stability analysis, identify four 
structural bottlenecks (fragmented data, weak generalization, limited 
interpretability and misaligned design objectives) and outline research 
directions to address each. Broad impact will require a transition from 
task-specific predictive tools towards system-level AI integration that 
preserves knowledge across the PSC research cycle.

The underlying difficulty is not primarily algorithmic. It reflects 
the difficulty of capturing the coupled and evolving relationships 
among chemistry, processing, device operation and degradation. 
These relationships are particularly demanding in PSCs, in which ion 
migration, crystallization dynamics, interfacial reactions and meta-
stability generate strong cross-scale dependencies that are difficult 
to capture using conventional data-driven models.
We refer to the research paradigm needed to address these 
dependencies as system-level AI integration: a mode of working in 
which knowledge generated at one stage of the PSC research cycle is 
continuously propagated, updated and reused to inform subsequent 
stages through coupled digital–physical feedback. What distinguishes 
it from conventional closed-loop optimization, which improves indi-
vidual tasks in isolation, is this continuous cross-stage propagation 
of transferable knowledge. Workflows that optimize only a single task 
without preserving and propagating information beyond it fall short 
of this definition.
This Review develops that argument in three parts. We first survey 
what AI has and has not delivered across materials discovery, device 
engineering, process optimization and stability analysis in PSCs, draw-
ing out a shared pattern of local acceleration but weak transferability. 
We then examine the four structural bottlenecks that limit broader 
impact (fragmented data, weak generalization, limited interpretability, 
and misalignment with practical design objectives) and the research 
directions that could address each. We close by sketching a route from 
today’s isolated workflows towards system-level AI integration across 
the PSC research life cycle.
AI capabilities and limits
AI has been applied at every stage of the PSC research cycle to address 
the challenges outlined earlier: materials discovery, device engineering, 
process optimization and stability analysis (Fig. 1). Across these stages, 
common strengths and limitations emerge. AI reliably identifies pat-
terns within well-defined systems and accelerates local optimization, 
but it extrapolates poorly beyond narrow compositional and process-
ing windows, offers weak transferability across device architectures 
and provides limited mechanistic insight. Considered together, these 
stage-specific limitations point to a shared cause: the fragmentation of 
AI workflows across an otherwise tightly coupled research pipeline. This 
fragmentation frames the case for system-level integration developed 
later in this Review.
Materials discovery and extrapolation
AI has proved most useful in compositional exploration. For proper-
ties such as the bandgap Eg, in which large and internally consistent 
datasets exist, models can map composition–property relationships 
more efficiently than experiment permits9–11 . These models enable 
navigation of multicomponent systems, including mixed-cation 
and mixed-halide compositions, without relying entirely on trial  
and error.
This approach has been extended to less conventional systems, 
including low-dimensional variants, structures modified by  ligands 
and Pb-free alternatives7,12–15 . AI is often used to relate molecular char-
acteristics such as ligand size or polarity to structural outcomes such 
as octahedral distortion, layer spacing or phase stability16– 18. These rela-
tionships are meaningful and, in some cases, provide useful  guidance 
for narrowing candidate materials.
Key points
 • Perovskite solar cell (PSC) deployment is bottlenecked by tightly 
coupled dependencies, not by single-parameter optimization. 
Composition governs processing dynamics, processing determines 
microstructure, and microstructure sets device performance, 
so improvements identified in one system rarely transfer to another.
 • Artificial intelligence (AI) accelerates PSC research locally but 
does not generalize. Across materials discovery, device engineering, 
process optimization and stability analysis, AI reliably identifies 
correlations and speeds up optimization within data-rich regimes;  
it does not yet extract mechanism-anchored insights that transfer 
across compositions, architectures or operating conditions.
 • The limitation is structural, not algorithmic. Four bottlenecks recur 
across AI applications in PSCs: fragmented and non-interoperable 
data, weak generalization across systems, limited interpretability with 
weak physical grounding, and misalignment between AI objectives and 
practical design constraints.
 • Addressing these bottlenecks requires integrated infrastructures, not 
more accurate individual models. Multimodal and multilevel datasets, 
physics-informed and explainable models and closed-loop coupling 
between AI predictions and experimental feedback each address one 
bottleneck; together, they provide the building blocks for a coupled 
research pipeline.
 • System-level AI integration is the direction of travel. The near-term 
shift is from local, task-specific AI towards frameworks in which 
knowledge is continuously propagated, updated and reused across 
the full PSC research cycle, reframing AI from a predictive tool into a 
participant in materials discovery, device optimization and reliability 
engineering.
Introduction
Halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have reached certified power con-
version efficiencies (PCEs)1 of 28%, and their tunable optoelectronic 
properties, solution processability and low fabrication costs 2 have 
made them one of the most active platforms in photovoltaic research. 
However, the path to industrial deployment remains constrained by 
instability under heat, moisture and illumination, by the presence of 
lead-based components and by a tightly coupled interplay among mate-
rial chemistry, processing conditions and device architecture3– 5. These 
couplings mean that improvements identified in one  composition, 
process or architecture rarely transfer to another.
Artificial intelligence (AI), spanning machine learning (ML), deep 
learning and data-driven optimization, has therefore been applied 
across the PSC research cycle, from bandgap Eg prediction and materi-
als screening to process optimization and device engineering6,7. Within 
well-defined systems and data-rich regimes, these approaches deliver 
measurable acceleration: they identify correlations that guide experi-
mental design, narrow candidate compositions and rank the variables 
that most strongly influence performance.
Progress in these areas has, nonetheless, been uneven. Most exist-
ing efforts remain narrowly scoped, relying on isolated predictive 
models with limited generalizability, weak links across composition,

Predictive performance, nonetheless, deteriorates rapidly once 
the chemistry moves outside the compositional range of the training 
data19. Unusual ligands, new B-site substitutions or competing phase 
behaviour introduces effects (defect formation, lattice instability 
and phase segregation) that are only weakly represented in existing 
datasets14,15. As a result, models extrapolate poorly and remain better 
at organizing known compositions than at identifying genuinely new 
materials. Most studies accordingly remain retrospective analyses of 
published data, with few examples in which AI predictions have guided 
experimental discovery of a new composition.
Device engineering and local design rules
At the device level, AI has helped identify the factors that most influ-
ence device performance, including energy level alignment, interfacial 
recombination and charge transport balance20,21. This has enabled more 
efficient screening of transport layers, interface modifiers and addi-
tive systems, particularly in architectures in which multiple variables 
interact nonlinearly22– 24.
In practice, AI often acts as a filter, narrowing candidate materials 
and highlighting design directions, for example, by identifying interfa-
cial treatments that suppress non-radiative recombination or additives 
that reduce defect density at grain boundaries24– 26. These insights can 
accelerate optimization within specific device architectures.
However, these findings rarely transfer across systems. Small varia-
tions in composition, interface chemistry or processing conditions can 
alter device behaviour, so that the predictive power of models trained 
on one dataset does not carry over to another. Correlations identified 
at the device level do not necessarily reflect underlying mechanisms 
such as defect passivation pathways or carrier recombination dynam-
ics. AI, therefore, supports local optimization but does not provide the 
mechanism-anchored design principles that would generalize across 
device platforms.
Process optimization and narrow optima
If there is a domain in which AI delivers immediate practical value, it 
is process optimization27. Fabrication of PSCs involves many coupled 
parameters, such as solvent choice, precursor ratios and annealing con-
ditions, and exploring this space experimentally is time-consuming28. 
AI can shorten this process by steering experiments towards regions 
likely to yield high performance29–33 .
Processing cannot, however, be reduced to a set of static param-
eters. Film formation depends on dynamic phenomena: nucleation 
behaviour, intermediate phase formation, solvent evaporation rates 
and grain growth pathways. The incorporation of in situ diagnostics 
has made this dependence explicit, revealing how small variations 
in these dynamics can lead to differences in morphology and device 
performance34,35.
The ‘optimal’ conditions identified by AI are often confined to 
a narrow window, so many AI-selected solutions are not robustly 
optimal but rather context-sensitive local optima. This narrowness 
helps explain why reproducibility across laboratories remains dif -
ficult and why scale-up to manufacturing continues to lag laboratory 
optimization.
Stability without mechanistic insight
Stability is arguably the most complex aspect of PSC research, and AI 
has been widely applied to it. By analysing time-dependent data such 
as photoluminescence evolution or spectral changes under stress, 
AI can identify which variables are most strongly associated with 
degradation36–38 .
These analyses help spot early warning signs: phase segregation 
under illumination, the onset of ion migration or the accumulation of 
defects that eventually lead to performance loss10,38. They also permit 
comparisons across different stress conditions, helping to rank the 
relative importance of heat, moisture and electric fields39–42 .

Most such analyses, nonetheless, operate at the level of correla-
tion. They identify which factors matter, but often do not capture how 
degradation pathways unfold at the microscopic level, for example, 
how ion migration interacts with defect formation or phase transitions 
over time. Combined with the absence of standardized long-term 
datasets, this limitation makes reliable lifetime prediction difficult. For 
now, AI organizes stability data better than it fully explains them, and 
generalizable lifetime prediction remains an open challenge.
Cross-domain limits and weak integration
Across materials discovery, device engineering, process optimization 
and stability analysis, a consistent limitation is evident: AI excels at 
high-dimensional correlation analysis and local optimization but does 
not deliver system-level, generalizable insights43. This is not merely a 
model shortcoming but a consequence of the tightly coupled nature 
of PSC systems, in which composition governs processing dynamics, 
processing defines microstructure, and microstructure determines 
device performance. Isolated treatment of these interconnected stages 
inevitably discards the mechanistic couplings that link them.
PSCs pose an unusually difficult challenge for AI because device 
behaviour emerges from tightly coupled and dynamically evolving 
processes that span multiple spatial and temporal scales. Ion migration 
continuously modifies local electric fields and defect distributions 
during operation, whereas crystallization pathways remain highly sensi-
tive to environmental fluctuations and processing history. Metastable 
phases, interfacial reactions and stress-induced structural evolution 
introduce non-equilibrium behaviour that is difficult to represent using 
static descriptors. As a result, correlations learnt from one dataset often 
become invalid when transferred across compositions, architectures 
or operating conditions.
Viewed from this perspective, the challenge is not simply to boost 
predictive accuracy, but to reframe research workflows around inte-
grated, cross-scale frameworks that unify data, models and experi -
ments. Such a paradigm shift will be as consequential for unlocking AI’s 
potential in PSC engineering as incremental algorithmic improvements.
Structural bottlenecks for AI
Reliable, widespread deployment of AI in PSC research is constrained by 
four interrelated bottlenecks (Fig. 2): fragmented data with weak inter-
operability, limited generalization across materials and processes, weak 
physical grounding of black-box models and misalignment between 
AI objectives and practical design constraints. Each is examined below 
alongside the research directions that could shift AI from a correlational 
tool towards a mechanistically grounded design partner.
Data gaps and interoperability limits
PSC research suffers from fragmented datasets with inconsistent 
formats, uncontrolled variability and limited public accessibility. 
Experimental data are often recorded in unstructured formats such 
as figures and supplementary files and lack essential metadata such as 
processing conditions, measurement protocols or degradation history.

Multimodal data integration challenges. PSC data are multimodal. 
Research should therefore not rely on simple correlation between 
literature-mined descriptors and performance. A well-formed dataset 
should integrate explicit data modalities (morphology images, spec-
troscopy, current–voltage and external quantum efficiency curves and 
ageing profiles) with structured metadata (fabrication parameters and 
scalar electrical metrics), so that models can learn the relationships 
among composition, process and performance 44. Monomodal data 
often capture only a subset of the relationships governing PSC behav-
iour and does not reflect fundamental physical principles. For example, 
materials with identical compositions can differ in morphology and 
stability because of varying processing conditions45– 47. Therefore, reli-
able predictions of performance and stability require joint modelling 
that combines composition, morphology, spectroscopic and electrical 
characterization.
We distinguish multimodal data from multisource and multilevel 
data. Multimodal data are fundamentally different forms of infor -
mation, such as morphology images, spectroscopy, electrical char -
acterization curves and ageing trajectories. By contrast, structured 
metadata provide contextual information about how each data record 
was obtained. Multisource and multilevel data capture information 
across different experimental pathways and physical scales. Effective 
PSC learning ultimately requires the integration of all three dimensions 
across composition, processing and device operation.
Multilevel data representation gaps.  PSC research requires the 
construction of rigorous, multilevel datasets to bridge computa -
tional and experimental feasibility48,49. Such datasets span four levels: 
atomic and molecular, thin-film, device and (as elaborated below) a 
knowledge-level representation that captures cross-level relation -
ships. Models trained solely on data from a single level tend to pro -
duce isolated black-box mappings, leading to mismatches between 
materials and experimental or device results 50– 52. By contrast, mul -
tilevel training incorporates cross-level physical constraints, which 
improves prediction accuracy and reveals mechanistic evolution44,53. 
For example, correlating the spectral signals of perovskites with mor-
phological heterogeneity can pinpoint the microscopic origins of 
device degradation54,55.
Standardization and dynamic updating.  A further data challenge 
is insufficient standardization and poor interoperability. In many 
subfields, chemical composition records lack a standardized format, 
making them difficult to convert into uniform atomic descriptors 56. 
Furthermore, multimodal data are often compressed into scalar 
values, resulting in the loss of information 54,57. Data integrity is com-
promised by sparse structures and inconsistent handling of miss -
ing values; in some databases, mixed notation and ambiguous field 
definitions can lead to ambiguity58. From a distributional perspective, 
literature-mining databases also suffer from positive bias and long-tail 
distribution issues — for example, an excess of data on TiO 2 electron 
transport layers, which can lead models to learn editorial preferences 
of the field rather than universal physical laws. Finally, static data -
set construction cannot keep pace with the rapid evolution of PSC 
technology56. Without dynamic update mechanisms, feature spaces 
lag behind current practice in the field.
Although the integrated multimodal, multilevel and standard -
ized database envisaged earlier remains conceptual, the continued 
emergence of monomodal databases offers a foundation on which 
such a resource can be built; concrete recommendations are set out 
in the Outlook section.
Weak generalization across systems
Most published models are system-specific and trained on narrow 
datasets, for example, laboratory-scale MAPbI3-based systems, result-
ing in weak generalization. These models cannot easily accommodate 
variations in dimensionality, chemical system and fabrication process. 
The challenge is not simply one of data quantity but the difficulty of pre-
serving physicochemical invariants across heterogeneous PSC systems. 
Data augmentation and transfer learning should therefore be viewed 
not as standalone solutions but as attempts to recover  transferable 
representations from incomplete observations.
Dataset augmentation limitations.  In perovskite research, sample 
scarcity makes it difficult to construct standardized databases, and 
this scarcity can lead to model underfitting and reduced prediction 
accuracy. T o address this scarcity, common data-level augmenta -
tion strategies used in perovskite research include conventional data 
transformation, generative adversarial networks and large-language-
model-based data generation technologies59,60. For example, elemental 
cation substitution at the  A-site and B-site of perovskite lattices is 
widely used to expand small original datasets and diversify sample 
coverage61.
Data augmentation has successfully increased dataset sizes. How-
ever, increasing dataset size does not necessarily improve dataset 
quality62. A challenge is that generated samples may satisfy statistical 
similarity to real data while violating fundamental physicochemical 
constraints. For PSC applications, charge neutrality, phase stability, 
tolerance factor requirements, synthetic accessibility and device rele-
vance should be considered during both data generation and validation 
(Table 1). Without such constraints, augmented datasets may introduce

Transfer learning and domain mismatch.  Although data augmen -
tation acts within a single system, transfer learning, which reuses a 
model trained on one system as the starting point for a related system, 
transfers general knowledge, structural patterns and features from 
the source domain to a different but related target domain. By using 
general structure–property relationships, transfer learning reduces 
the computational and time costs of training from scratch.
However, transfer learning has yielded limited results in perovskite 
materials research so far and can suffer from negative transfer caused 
by cross-domain heterogeneity 63. In a typical failure case, models 
trained on the dataset of lead–halide  2D perovskites processed by 
conventional solution methods produce unreliable predictions when 
applied directly to Sn-based or Bi-based perovskite systems61. The dis-
tinct ionic radii, valence electron configurations and lattice-distortion 
characteristics of the two families break the shared structure–property 
correlation the model has learnt64. Similarly, models optimized for 2D 
perovskite architectures often do not transfer to other architectures, 
because charge transport requirements and bonding mechanisms vary 
across device structures65.
More fundamentally, transfer failure often arises because mod-
els learn dataset-specific statistical regularities rather than invari -
ant physicochemical relationships. When the dominant degradation 
mechanisms, crystallization pathways, defect chemistries or interfacial 
interactions differ between source and target domains, correlations 
learnt from one system become unreliable predictors in another. As a 
result, models that perform well within a narrowly defined materials 
family or processing protocol frequently fail when transferred across 
compositions, device architectures or operating conditions. This chal-
lenge is particularly severe in PSC research because device performance 
emerges from coupled interactions among composition, processing 
history, microstructure and operational conditions, many of which 
are poorly represented in existing datasets. A shared data space must 
therefore be carefully designed to avoid mismatches in structure and 
features and to eliminate systematic experimental biases. The pre -
cursor space produced by transfer learning still requires validation 
by small-scale, high-throughput experimentation to quantify gains in 
prediction accuracy.
Interpretability and physical grounding
A criticism of AI in materials science is its black-box nature. High-  
performing models often provide little insight into why a given 
prediction is made. In the PSC context, this both constrains 
mechanism-guided design of materials and devices and impedes the 
integration of ML with density functional theory (DFT), device model-
ling and process simulation, and it reduces the reliability of research 
results and inter-laboratory reproducibility. Three responses to these 
limitations are considered below: physics-informed ML (PIML), which 
embeds known physical laws into the model’s architecture or train-
ing objective; explainable AI, which exposes which input features 
drive a model’s predictions; and the joint use of models with physical 
 simulation.
Physics-informed modelling strategies. In PSC research, the main 
barrier to interpretable AI is that purely data-driven models can -
not account for the strong nonlinear couplings among the dynamic 
processes that occur inside a PSC. PIML is particularly suited to PSC 
systems, because it can embed lattice dynamics, carrier transport, ion 
migration and defect physics into neural networks, so that models can 
infer causal relationships among processing history, microstructure 
evolution, device operation and long-term stability, rather than relying 
on empirical correlations.
However, multiscale physical coupling remains conceptual in 
practice. This is because many key phenomena in perovskites evolve 
across multiple spatial and temporal scales and cannot be fully char-
acterized by a single physical constraint or statistical correlation. For 
example, ion migration continuously modifies local electric fields and 
interfacial energetics during device operation, whereas crystallization 
pathways determine defect distributions and microstructure forma-
tion during film growth. In addition, degradation processes emerge 
from the coupled evolution of defects, strain, phase segregation and 
interfacial reactions under external stresses.
Phase transitions, halide segregation, lattice reconstruction and 
black-phase degradation are rare events involving bond-breaking 
and restructuring, so that the physical loss function of PIML cannot 
precisely constrain the kinetic pathways. Organic–inorganic hybrid 
perovskites also involve covalent, ionic and van der Waals interactions, 
for which no unified analytical physical prior is available.
Explainability and its limitations.  A range of explainable AI tech -
niques, such as Shapley additive explanations, have been used to ana-
lyse feature importance and decision logic, thereby increasing human 
trust in models64. Although these methods, and their combinations 
with other approaches, appear to provide interpretable insights for 
optimizing PSC performance and exploring underlying mechanisms, 
long-standing puzzles remain unresolved20,37,65.
Existing mechanistic studies have largely focused on rationalizing 
experimental observations within established frameworks, whereas 
fundamentally new mechanistic perspectives remain scarce. For exam-
ple, a particular halide ion has been reported to dominate both the 
bandgap66 and the perovskite adsorption energy67. This convergence 
on a single feature may reflect subjective feature selection or the omis-
sion of features judged unimportant a priori. Future opportunities 
may therefore lie in applying explainable AI to phenomena that remain 
difficult to probe experimentally, including metastable ion migra -
tion pathways, high-temperature degradation and crystallization 
dynamics, in which AI may help reveal hidden variables and causal 
relationships39.
Furthermore, biases arising from correlations between element 
features should also be reduced68. Explainable AI should not be limited 
to explaining ‘material design’; explaining ‘model design’ will improve 
the accuracy of model predictions.
Model and simulation integration. Integrating ML models with DFT 
and time-dependent DFT simulations has become a standard work -
flow in perovskite calculations 69,70. Typically, molecular screening 
from source databases to intermediate or synthetic databases relies 
on random selection or feature engineering to identify meaningful 
descriptors22,71; however, this approach remains constrained by the sub-
jective judgement of the researcher. By contrast, molecular descriptors 
derived from DFT calculations in intermediate databases shift the focus 
from human-defined macroscopic features to microscopic quantities.
Automating the evaluation of DFT convergence, the selection 
of functionals and the diagnosis of strong correlations could reduce 
human intervention in ML and may become a core component of 
autonomous design. Although cross-validating DFT results against

Misalignment with design objectives
Many AI models in PSC research target only maximizing PCE. How-
ever, industrial feasibility requires balancing PCE against long-term 
operational stability, toxicity and environmental impact, and scalability 
against material costs and supply-chain constraints. Multi-objective 
optimization (MOO) frameworks that map trade-offs on a Pareto front, 
together with lifecycle-aware AI, are needed to move PSCs from the 
laboratory to commercialization.
Multi-objective optimization frameworks.  MOO has been widely 
applied in perovskite research because it addresses problems that 
require coordinated screening of multiple metrics, including device 
design, interface control, process sensitivity and performance cou -
pling. It reduces experimental and simulation costs while streamlining 
optimization from material screening to device fabrication 72. Com-
bined with high-throughput experiments and ML-assisted experi -
mental design, MOO supports systematic frameworks for identifying 
component and doping schemes that balance PCE and long-term 
stability73.
However, MOO’s decision-making depends on human-defined 
objectives and offers limited insight into the structure–property mech-
anisms of perovskites. Furthermore, MOO may remain confined to 
laboratory research, as there is no unified quantitative standard for 
weighting multiple objectives in industrial applications.
Lifecycle-aware design strategies. When assessing the market poten-
tial and industrialization progress of PSCs, it is essential to consider 
performance, stability, cost, environmental impact and device lifespan. 
AI that incorporates lifecycle metrics enables sustainability assess -
ment across PCE, toxicity and environmental burden43. For example, 
the green solvents γ-pentyl acetate and ethyl acetate can reduce the 
safety risks and costs of traditional solvent systems while maintaining 
a high PCE43,74. From a full-lifecycle perspective, PSCs based on such 
solvent systems can become competitive with Si photovoltaics and 
other renewable energy sources.
Although lifecycle-aware AI remains conceptual in the perovskite 
field, it is central to moving from the laboratory to commercialization75. 
Its development requires the synergistic advancement of data, models 
and MOO, together with a unified digital foundation and industry 
standards spanning the full chain from research and development to 
recycling. It must also integrate cross-scale physical mechanisms to 
achieve closed-loop management across the full life cycle.
These four bottlenecks — fragmented data, weak generalization, 
limited interpretability and misaligned objectives — are interconnected 
rather than independent. Each ultimately reflects the same underlying 
challenge: current AI workflows cannot represent or learn the coupled 
processes that span composition, processing, device physics and deg-
radation across scales. This shared cause motivates the integrated AI 
frameworks discussed next.
Outlook
It is tempting to attribute progress in AI for PSC research to increas-
ingly powerful models. In practice, however, many of the limitations 
discussed earlier do not arise from modelling capability alone; they 
reflect a mismatch between how data are used, how models are trained 
and how experiments are carried out. The future of AI in PSC research 
will therefore be shaped less by incremental algorithmic improvement 
than by frameworks that integrate data, physics, experimentation and 
operational feedback.
Progress will therefore depend not only on model architecture 
but also on the quality, diversity and interoperability of experimental 
datasets and on knowledge transfer across all PSC subtasks in both the 
physical and digital realms.
Dynamic and physics-aware modelling
Processing in PSC systems is inherently dynamic; however, it is often 
treated as a static optimization problem. In reality, crystallization 
pathways, intermediate phases and solvent dynamics evolve over time 
and are highly sensitive to environmental conditions6. Small fluctua-
tions in humidity or temperature can alter nucleation behaviour and 
grain growth, producing different defect distributions and device 
performance76. Such effects are difficult to capture with models that 
rely only on fixed input parameters.
Moving forward, incorporating time-dependent and environment-

Closed-loop AI–experimental integration
A related direction is the integration of AI with experimental 
workflows77. Rather than treating prediction and validation as separate 
steps, these approaches create iterative cycles in which models guide 
experiments and are continuously updated by new data.
In PSC research, such closed-loop workflows could bridge stages 
that are currently separated: for example, composition design, process-
ing optimization and device evaluation78. Implementing them remains 
challenging. Experimental platforms need to execute precisely and 
provide reliable data, whereas feedback models must adapt as new 
information becomes available. At least in the near term, the goal is 
less about full automation than about strengthening the feedback loop 
between decision-making and physical validation.
T owards system-level AI integration
T o realize the potential of AI in PSC research, the field must move beyond 
isolated prediction tasks towards integrated, iterative workflows that 
link data maturity, model development and experimental validation 
into a continuous feedback system. Here, we outline a two-part road-
map: first, a conceptual four-stage progression of AI-enabled research 
paradigms (Table 2), and second, the transition from local closed-loop 
workflows to a global system-level framework (Fig. 3).
Four-stage evolution of AI paradigms. The progression outlined sub-
sequently reflects a progressive increase in the coupling among data, 
models and experiments within PSC research. The progression is con-
ceptual rather than chronological and represents the expanding role 
of AI from retrospective analysis to increasingly active  participation 
in experimental decision-making.
At present, most work remains focused on data-driven predic -
tion. Models are trained on curated datasets to estimate device per -
formance or material properties, often achieving good retrospective 
accuracy within well-defined systems. However, these approaches 
remain  limited by the scope and quality of available data.
A second stage involves design-oriented tasks, in which AI is used 
not only to predict outcomes but also to suggest candidate materials 
or processing conditions. Here, the focus begins to shift towards incor-
porating constraints such as stability, toxicity or manufacturability, 
beyond purely performance-driven optimization. However, candidate 
validation still relies largely on human intervention.
A third stage introduces local closed-loop workflows, either 
through robotic experimentation or through digital  multi-agent 
systems 38,79– 81. Although these approaches shorten the distance 
between decision-making and validation, they remain confined to indi-
vidual tasks or platforms82. Looking ahead, a global autonomous PSC 
framework would generate, test and refine hypotheses continuously

From local to global integration. Existing autonomous AI paradigms 
differ primarily in the scope of knowledge propagation: robotic experi-
mentation, closed-loop optimization and self-driving laboratories 
remain largely confined to individual tasks or platforms, whereas 
system-level AI integration enables continuous knowledge transfer 
across the entire PSC research life cycle.
The motivation for such integration is strong in PSC systems, 
in which performance emerges from tightly coupled interactions 
among composition, processing and device architecture 83. At pre-
sent, most local closed-loop AI–experiment workflows are closed 
around a defined task, objective, platform or protocol. Unlike local 
closed-loop optimization, PSC research requires AI to preserve cou -
pled relationships across materials chemistry, processing, interfaces, 
device physics and stability. A system-level framework therefore links 
hypothesis generation, experimentation and model updating through 
continuous cross-stage knowledge propagation 84: models propose 
candidate materials or processing strategies, experiments evaluate 
their performance under controlled conditions and the resulting data 
are used to refine subsequent predictions. Over successive iterations, 
this process can improve both predictive accuracy and experimental 
efficiency. The challenge is therefore not to close a simple loop but 
to determine how much of the PSC research workflow the loop can 
meaningfully connect.

Glossary
2D perovskite
A layered variant in which insulating 
organic spacer molecules separate 
perovskite octahedral slabs, giving 
increased moisture tolerance but 
reduced charge transport.
A-site and B-site
The two cation positions in the ABX2 
perovskite lattice: the A-site hosts a large 
cation and the B-site a smaller cation.
Bandgap
The minimum energy required 
to promote an electron from a 
semiconductor’s valence band to 
its conduction band. In perovskites, 
it determines which wavelengths of 
sunlight are absorbed.
Black-phase degradation
Loss of the photoactive black perovskite 
phase through conversion to a 
non-photoactive polymorph.
Crystallization pathway
The sequence of nucleation, 
intermediate-phase formation and grain 
growth by which a perovskite thin film 
transitions from precursor solution to 
solid film.
Electron transport layer
Thin layers adjacent to the  
perovskite absorber that selectively 
extract electrons and block the 
opposite carrier.
Generative adversarial 
network
A pair of neural networks (generator 
and discriminator) trained against 
each other to produce synthetic 
data samples resembling a target 
distribution.
High-throughput 
experimentation
Parallelized experimental workflows 
in which many samples are prepared 
and characterized in an automated 
pipeline.
Ligand
An organic molecule bonded to 
the perovskite lattice or a colloidal 
nanocrystal surface, used to tune 
structural, electronic or interfacial 
properties.
Metastable phase
A structural configuration of 
the perovskite that is not the 
thermodynamic ground state but is 
kinetically trapped and can persist 
under operating conditions.
Multi-agent system
A software framework in which 
multiple autonomous AI agents 
cooperate or negotiate to perform 
tasks that a single agent cannot  
easily handle.
Non-radiative recombination
Loss of a photogenerated electron– 
hole pair through processes that do 
not emit a photon; a primary limit on 
solar cell voltage.
Octahedral distortion
Deviation of the BX6 coordination 
octahedron from ideal geometry,  
which shifts electronic structure  
and phase stability.
Pareto front
The set of solutions to a multi- 
objective problem in which no 
objective can be improved without 
worsening another.
Passivation
Surface or interface treatment that 
neutralizes defects capable of trapping 
charge carriers, thereby suppressing 
non-radiative recombination.
Phase segregation
Reversible or irreversible separation  
of a mixed-halide perovskite  
into iodide-rich and bromide- 
rich domains under illumination  
or bias, which alters the local 
bandgap.
Tolerance factor
A dimensionless geometric ratio 
predicting whether a given combination 
of A, B and X ions will form a stable 
perovskite structure.

The value of such systems does not lie in full automation but 
in improved decision quality. By systematically linking hypothesis 
generation with experimental validation, closed-loop approaches 
can reduce reliance on trial and error and help identify more robust 
design strategies78. For PSCs, feedback must also capture variables 
that strongly affect reproducibility, including processing history, 
environmental conditions, measurement protocols and device-to-
device variability. Progress towards system-level AI is likely to be 
incremental38,72,73,85–87  (Table 3): near-term advances will rely on tight 
integration of local digital and physical loops, ultimately supporting 
a global framework that continuously propagates knowledge across 
the PSC research life cycle88,89.
The future impact of AI in PSC research will depend less on more 
sophisticated algorithms than on closed-loop AI–experiment inte -
gration within adaptive system-level frameworks that preserve the 
coupled relationships among chemistry, processing, device physics 
and operational stability. Such a convergence may transform AI from a 
predictive tool into an active participant in the discovery, optimization 
and reliability engineering of PSCs.
